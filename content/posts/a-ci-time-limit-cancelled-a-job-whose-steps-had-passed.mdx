**Short answer:** my reading is that a time limit on a CI job is a claim about how long the job can take, and the job's own history is the evidence for it. If the limit sits just above the slowest runs in which the job passed, a slower run can trip it, and a job that has done all its work can still be recorded as cancelled. Anything downstream that reads cancelled as not green then turns that into a deploy workflow run that does not deploy. My reading is that a limit belongs above the slowest run you can measure, with margin, and with the measurement written next to the number. Northsun's records describe this happening on 9 October 2026. They record a change that raised the limit and kept the deploy workflow's rule that a cancelled run is not green.

**Terms, as this post uses them.** A *time limit* is the longest a CI job may run before the CI service stops it; GitHub's setting for it is called `timeout-minutes`. *Cancelled* is the conclusion a job or run gets when it is stopped, whether by its time limit or by a newer push. A *run* is one execution of a workflow for a commit, and an *attempt* is one try of that run, so a re-run is a second attempt. The *deploy workflow* is the workflow that runs after CI and decides whether a commit is deployed, and its *gate* is the job that makes that decision. *Green* means the run concluded success. A *required workflow* is a workflow whose completed run the deploy workflow waits for. An *annotation* is the note GitHub attaches to a job's record, here saying why the job ended. A *task row* is the documentation file that tracks one task in the repository's build plan.

**Status of what this describes:** a change to a CI configuration file, with three documentation files (among them the task row and the deployment log entry), in Northsun's private repository, PR #1702, merged to its main branch on 9 October 2026. GitHub's run records show that the CI run on main for the merge commit concluded success, and that the deploy-workflow run triggered by that CI run concluded success. That workflow can also conclude success when it deliberately deploys nothing, and I did not check production, so this post says merged, and that a deploy workflow concluded success, and nothing about what is served. ## What happened

**The job and its limit.** Northsun's CI workflow has a job that runs a verification script. I leave out what it verifies. The job had a time limit, set in the workflow file. The comment that the fix adds says that the job's main step alone took most of that limit on each of the runs it cites in which the job passed, on top of the job's setup. Adding the two puts those runs at most of the limit, the slowest of them at nearly all of it; that sum is my arithmetic from the comment's figures, which I leave out. So the limit sat only just above how long the job took on the passing runs the comment cites, and a slightly slower run would exceed it.

**The day.** On 9 October 2026 the limit cancelled that job on more than one run: pull-request runs, and the first attempt of CI on a commit on main, the merge of PR #1652. The log entry says the re-run on that commit tripped the limit too, and GitHub's record shows that the CI run's second attempt concluded `cancelled`.

**What the records show about the cause.** A newer push can also cancel a CI run, so I read below the run level. The annotation on each cancelled verification job I read says the job exceeded its maximum execution time. In the job records for both attempts on main (the second attempt repeats the first attempt's records for the other jobs), that job is the only one not recorded as success, and every step in it, including the verification step, is recorded as success. I also read the log of one of the pull-request jobs, and the verification step in it printed its passing message before the job ended. So for this job the limit did not cut the verification off part way through. The steps had passed, and the job was recorded as cancelled all the same.

**What a cancelled run meant downstream.** A cancelled job made the CI run cancelled, as the conclusion of the run for that commit shows. The deploy workflow starts when the run of either of the two required workflows completes, and its header says what it does with each result. A red, cancelled or timed-out completion runs a job that reports a required check was not green. The gate fails when a required workflow was cancelled while main still points at that commit, and it calls a cancelled run superseded, which is not a failure, once main has moved past the commit. Nothing had moved past this commit: the merge of #1702 is its direct successor on main, and it landed after the runs described here. The log entry quotes the deploy workflow's message: "CI was cancelled … nothing superseded this commit, and nothing will deploy it. Re-run CI …". The deploy-workflow runs I saw for that commit all concluded `failure`, and GitHub's job records show the deploy job skipped in each. Those started by the CI run's attempts failed in the job that reports a required check was not green. One was started by the completion of the other required workflow: its gate job succeeded while CI was still running, and its watcher job, which waits for the commit to be deployed, failed after CI concluded cancelled. The log entry says that, as of when it was written, the commit stayed merged and not deployed. I did not check production.

**The fix.** One line raised the job's time limit, and a comment above it records the measurement and the cancellations. The log entry says what the change did not do. It left the deploy workflow alone, so a cancelled run is still not green, and the entry says that rule stands. It moved the limit to where the measured step fits with margin, instead of making the step faster first. It defers two questions: why the verification step takes as long as it does, and whether the job should be a required check. After the merge, GitHub's records show the CI run on main for the merge commit concluded success, and the deploy-workflow run triggered by that CI run concluded success. The commit whose CI runs were cancelled, c889a22c, is an ancestor of that merge commit in git history; I make no claim about what any deploy run shipped. I have not looked at enough runs after the change to say whether the new limit is enough.

**Another limit, earlier the same day.** The task row records another job in the same file whose limit was exceeded after its typecheck step had passed. The comment beside the raised limit says the test steps timed out, so that job was stopped while its tests were running. The task row says the tool that lands pull requests read the cancelled job as red and stopped. That limit was raised in a different pull request, #1697, merged earlier that day. I read that change's diff for the limit and its comment. The same pull request changed a test database setting, and its decision record gives the longer run that setting causes as a second reason for the larger limit; this post covers neither. The two cancelled jobs differ: the verification job had finished its steps, and the other was cut off while its work was running. A cancelled conclusion looks the same for both.

**Why a list of passing runs leaves the slow ones out.** A job with a limit can only pass if it finishes inside the limit, so a list of its passing runs holds only the runs that fit. The runs that were stopped are not on it, and a stopped run's recorded duration is about the limit itself, which says only that the run was slower. The script below shows that effect with invented numbers.

## The script

It uses only Node built-ins and was run on Node 22.22.0 on Linux. It invents a job: a fixed setup time plus a main step whose speed varies from run to run, with about 12% of runs slow ones in sections 1 to 3. Its limit sits just above the slowest ordinary run and below the slow ones. Section 4 uses a different toy with no slow mode. Every number in it is made up, and the 12% and the 0.8 are assumptions, not measurements of anything. It does not model any real CI service. Its deploy rules are a simplified version of some of the outcomes the deploy workflow's header describes.

```js
// cap.mjs: a CI time limit set just above a job's passing runs.
// A stand-in, not Northsun code. Every number here is invented. Run: node cap.mjs   (Node built-ins only)

function rng(seed) {                       // a small seeded generator, so every run prints the same thing
  return () => { seed = (seed + 0x6D2B79F5) | 0; let t = Math.imul(seed ^ (seed >>> 15), 1 | seed);
    t = (t + Math.imul(t ^ (t >>> 7), 61 | t)) ^ t; return ((t ^ (t >>> 14)) >>> 0) / 4294967296; };
}
const SETUP = 12, WORK = 70;                // seconds: job setup, and the main step at its usual speed
const ordinary = (r) => 0.92 + 0.2 * r();                                  // an ordinary run: 0.92 to 1.12 times the usual speed
const runFactor = (r) => ordinary(r) * (r() < 0.12 ? 1.45 : 1);             // and 12% of runs are slow ones, 1.45 times slower
const runtime = (f) => SETUP + WORK * f;
const pct = (xs, p) => xs.slice().sort((a, b) => a - b)[Math.min(xs.length - 1, Math.floor(p * xs.length))];
const fmt = (x) => x.toFixed(1);

const r = rng(7);
const history = Array.from({ length: 2000 }, () => runtime(runFactor(r)));   // what the job would take with no limit at all

console.log("1. what a limit hides. The same job, 2000 runs, no limit: median " + fmt(pct(history, 0.5)) + " s, slowest " + fmt(Math.max(...history)) + " s.");
const CAP0 = 91;
const green = history.filter((t) => t <= CAP0);
console.log(`   With a ${CAP0} s limit, ${green.length} runs pass and ${history.length - green.length} are cancelled. A list of the passing runs shows:`);
console.log(`   median ${fmt(pct(green, 0.5))} s, slowest ${fmt(Math.max(...green))} s, ${Math.round(100 * green.filter((t) => t > 0.9 * CAP0).length / green.length)}% of them above 90% of the limit.`);
console.log("   The cancelled runs are not in that list, so the list looks like a job that fits.");

console.log("\n2. cancelled share by where the limit sits (the same 2000 runs):");
for (const [why, cap] of [["at the typical time", pct(history, 0.5)], ["just above the ordinary runs", CAP0], ["1.5 x the slowest run ever seen", 1.5 * Math.max(...history)]]) {
  const cancelled = history.filter((t) => t > cap).length;
  console.log(`   limit ${fmt(cap).padStart(6)} s (${why.padEnd(34)}) cancels ${String(cancelled).padStart(4)} of ${history.length}`);
}

console.log("\n3. what 'cancelled' means to the deploy step. The conclusion is the same whether the job's steps had finished or not:");
const gate = (conclusion, mainStillHere, policy) => conclusion === "success" ? "deploy"
  : conclusion === "cancelled" ? (policy === "loosened" ? "deploy" : mainStillHere ? "refuse (not green)" : "superseded (the newer commit's own runs decide)") : "refuse";
console.log("   " + "run ended".padEnd(48) + "gate: cancelled is not green".padEnd(50) + "gate: cancelled counts as done");
for (const [label, c, here, finished] of [["success", "success", true, true], ["cancelled, newest commit, steps unfinished", "cancelled", true, false],
  ["cancelled, newest commit, steps had finished", "cancelled", true, true], ["cancelled, main moved on", "cancelled", false, false]]) {
  const note = c === "cancelled" && here ? (finished ? "   <- strict refuses a commit whose steps had passed" : "   <- loose ships a commit whose steps never finished") : "";
  console.log("   " + label.padEnd(48) + gate(c, here, "strict").padEnd(50) + gate(c, here, "loosened") + note);
}

console.log("\n4. does a re-run help? A different toy: no slow mode, run times spread evenly from 76 s to 90 s, and a limit of 86 s inside that spread. Two attempts on a commit whose first attempt tripped it:");
const CAP4 = 86;
const trial = (rho) => { const rr = rng(11); let first = 0, both = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < 20000; i++) { const a = ordinary(rr); const b = rho * a + (1 - rho) * ordinary(rr);
    if (runtime(a) > CAP4) { first++; if (runtime(b) > CAP4) both++; } }
  return `${Math.round(100 * both / first)}% of the second attempts trip too`; };
console.log("   if each attempt gets independent conditions:       " + trial(0));
console.log("   if the slowness is shared between attempts (0.8):  " + trial(0.8));
```

What it shows, with its limits. Section 1: with a limit just above the ordinary runs, the passing runs are a sample that leaves out the slow ones. Their slowest is 90.4 s against a 91 s limit, and 60% of them sit above 90% of the limit, while the 240 slow runs that were cancelled appear nowhere in that list. My reading: passing runs that end close to the limit are a sign the limit is inside the range; the 90% is only the cut I chose for the toy. Section 2: a limit at the typical time cancels about half the runs, a limit just above the ordinary runs cancels the 240 slow ones, and a limit at 1.5 times the slowest run ever seen cancels none. The 1.5 is only a number I picked for the toy, and not a recommendation. Section 3: the deploy rules cannot tell a run stopped part way from one whose steps had finished. A rule that treats cancelled as done ships the run whose steps never finished, and a rule that treats it as not green refuses the run whose steps had passed. The not-green rule also lets a newer commit supersede an older one. The table is the script's own rules written out, and is not a simulation.

Section 4 is a different toy, with no slow mode: run times spread evenly across a range, and a limit inside it. If each attempt gets independent conditions, 32% of second attempts trip, which is about the base rate. If the slowness is shared between attempts, 82% do. My reading: in this toy a failed re-run is likelier when the slowness is shared, so one failed re-run shifts the odds but does not settle which case you are in. The 0.8 is an assumption, and the toy does not measure how often a real re-run fails.

For the real case, the log entry says the re-run was cancelled too. That fits shared slowness, and it fits a job whose ordinary duration straddles its limit, and it is one case.

## What the change left alone

The deploy workflow did not change. The log entry says a cancelled run is not green and that this rule stands. My reading, since the record gives no reason beyond that the rule stands: section 3 of the script shows what the rule protects against, and the other job above is a real example of a cancelled job that was cut off while its work was running. The re-run advice did not change either: the deploy workflow's own message tells the operator to re-run CI, and the records I read do not discuss whether the slowness was shared between the attempts.

The change did not make the job faster, and the record defers the question of why the verification step takes as long as it does. It did not decide whether the job should be a required check at all, which is the other deferred question. It did not change the deploy workflow or any application code. What the change addressed was the limit, so the limit moved.

## How to check your own jobs

These are rules of thumb, not measurements. List the durations of recent runs of each job that has a limit. A cancelled run's duration is about the limit itself, a lower bound, so to see the real top of the range, record durations without a limit or raise it for a while. Put the slowest you can see next to the limit. If passing runs end close to the limit, the limit is probably inside the range. Set it above the slowest with margin, and write the measurement in a comment above the number, as the fixed file does. Then find everything downstream that reads cancelled: a deploy workflow, a merge queue, a landing tool, a bot. Ask what each one does with it, and whether it can tell a run stopped part way from one whose steps had finished. Last, decide what a re-run means when a limit trips. If the slowness is shared between attempts, a second failure is likely. If each attempt gets different conditions, a second failure comes at about the base rate. One pair of attempts does not settle which applies.

## FAQ

### Why not treat a cancelled run as success, so the deploy goes ahead?

My reading, not the record's: a cancelled conclusion does not say whether the job's steps had finished. In the verification job they had, and in the other job they had not, and both ended as cancelled. The deploy workflow's header gives cases where a cancelled run is not a failure, such as when a newer commit has superseded it, and then the newer commit's own runs decide. For the newest commit, a rule that accepted cancelled would sometimes ship a commit whose steps were stopped part way through. The log entry gives no reason beyond that the rule stands.

### Why not just re-run it?

You can, and the log entry says it was done and tripped the limit again, which is one case. My reading: if the job lives at its limit and the conditions are shared between attempts, a second failure is likely and a pass is luck, so raise the limit first and look at the job's speed separately. In the script's toy, a failed re-run is likelier under shared slowness (82%) than under independent conditions (32%), so one failed re-run shifts the odds but does not settle which case you are in. Section 4 of the script shows both cases with invented numbers.

### Should a CI job have a time limit at all?

I read nothing that weighs removing limits, so this is my reading and not the records'. A limit stops a hung job from running for hours, and that is worth having. My reading is that the question is where to put it: above the slow end of the job's real range, with the reason written beside it.

### Is it deployed?

A deploy workflow run concluded success after the merge. That workflow can also conclude success when it deliberately deploys nothing, and I did not check production, so I say merged.
