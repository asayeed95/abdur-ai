**Short answer:** to assert that eight times the input costs at most a bound times the time, do not divide two raw timings. Warm both sizes up, take the fastest of several interleaved runs per size, put a floor under the small time, and choose a bound between linear (about eight times) and quadratic (about sixty-four times). Then know what you bought. Northsun's helper does this, reports it stable under heavy synthetic load, and states the price: a quadratic that costs less than the bound times the floor at the large size is not caught. In the toy below, the floor and not the fastest-of-nine is what kept a correct scanner green under heavy load, and inputs big enough that the floor never binds worked nearly as well.

**Status of what this describes:** a test-only change in Northsun's private repository, merged on 5 October 2026 as pull request 1549. Merged is not deployed, and this change deploys nothing: it changes no served code, and nothing here is a claim about how any service behaves. The script in this post is a stand-in, described below. It times a scanner written for the demo, on a four-core sandbox machine, with busy-loop processes as the load, and its numbers are its own.

**Who did the work:** Northsun's agents, not me, with one thing I cannot separate out: the merge commit carries my account name, and the record does not say whether I or an automation merged it. The failure was reported and the fix assigned inside Northsun's agent workflow, and a build agent (an AI session) wrote the helper and changed the tests. The script and the runs of it in this post were done on 7 October 2026 by the AI session that drafts these write-ups, not by me and not by the build agent. Where this post says "Northsun", it means that project and its agents.

**Terms, as this post uses them.** A *ratio arm* is a test that times a piece of code on an input, times it on an input eight times larger, and asserts that the large time is at most a *bound* times the small one. A *flake* is a failure with no defect in the code under test. *Contention* is other work competing for the CPU. A *busy loop* is a process that does nothing but spin, used here to create contention. A *floor* is a lower limit put under the small time before dividing. *Fastest of nine* means nine timed runs of the same input, keeping the lowest.

## What happened

Northsun is the memory and enrichment layer for AI agents.

Northsun's test suite guards its text scanners against an accidental quadratic cost with ratio arms. Linear code costs about eight times more on eight times the input, and quadratic code about sixty-four times, so a bound between the two separates them without any absolute number to tune per machine.

One such arm failed main's push CI on a loaded CI runner, and the failure blocked the deploy of main. The task row says so, and adds that two earlier reviews had already found the arm flaky, where it read just above its bound under load. The scanner was not defective. The record's diagnosis is about the measurement.

The old arms timed each size three times, the small size first and then the large size, with no warm-up. They took the median of each, divided the large median by the small median floored at a fraction of a millisecond, and asserted that the result stayed under a bound, 20 for the arms the record describes. That is the shape the arms had in the merge's first parent, and it is the shape the toy below calls the old arm.

## Why a ratio of two timings is a coin

The record puts it in one sentence: under contention this is a ratio of two noisy numbers with a sub-millisecond denominator, so one slow run of the large size, or a small size that reads low, crosses the bound with no defect in the code. It adds the observation that carries the whole fix: contention only ever adds time to a run. A run can be slower than the code's true cost but not faster, so the slow runs say something about the machine and the fastest runs say the most about the code.

## What the replacement does

The record describes one shared helper, now used by every ratio arm in that file, in four moves.

1. **Warm both sizes up.** Each size runs twice, unmeasured, so compilation and first-call allocation are not in the numbers.
2. **Fastest of nine, interleaved.** Each size is the fastest of nine measured runs, run in the order small, large, small, large, so a burst of load lands on both sizes.
3. **A floor under the small time.** The denominator is the larger of the small time and a floor of a few milliseconds. A reading that small is mostly timer and scheduler noise, and the floor means the large input may cost the bound times the floor before the arm goes red, which every linear scanner in the file clears by a wide margin.
4. **One bound.** Eight times the input may cost at most thirty times the time, for every arm.

The helper returns its measurement, so an arm can add the absolute ceiling it already kept behind an opt-in flag, and those ceilings are unchanged.

The record's evidence is of two kinds, both as the record reports them and not re-run for this post. First, under heavy synthetic contention the old arms failed in a minority of runs and the new arms in none. It also says that busy loops alone, one per core, did not slow the file enough to reproduce the flake, and that more contention than that was needed. Second, a quadratic planted in each scanner family turned red every arm that covers that family, and only those arms.

## A toy on a local machine

Northsun's scanners are private, so this is a toy. It has a linear scanner, the same scanner with a planted quadratic defect, and the old arm and each of the changes above, added one at a time. It reads the arms three ways: false alarms on the correct scanner under rising contention, defects the arms miss, and a stable ratio reached without a floor. It needs Node and nothing else. It starts its own busy-loop processes, up to four per core, and kills them when it ends, and a full run takes several minutes.

```js
// demo.mjs: why a ratio-of-timings test flakes under CPU contention, and what the fix costs.
// Run: node demo.mjs   (takes several minutes; it starts its own busy-loop processes and kills them when it ends)
import { spawn } from "node:child_process";
import { performance } from "node:perf_hooks";
import os from "node:os";

const CORES = os.availableParallelism(), TRIALS = 100;
const text = (n) => "4111 1111 1111 1111 x".repeat(Math.ceil(n / 21)).slice(0, n);

// The code under test: a linear scanner, and the same scanner with a planted quadratic defect:
// for every character it also re-reads a `share` of the input, so its cost grows with the square of the input.
const scan = (s) => { const m = new Map(); for (let i = 0; i < s.length; i++) { const c = s.charCodeAt(i); m.set(c, (m.get(c) ?? 0) + 1); } return m.size; };
const withDefect = (share) => (s) => { let acc = scan(s); const inner = Math.floor(s.length * share); for (let i = 0; i < s.length; i++) for (let j = 0; j < inner; j++) acc ^= s.charCodeAt(j); return acc; };

// The property under test: an input 8x larger costs at most `bound` times as much.
// The small input is sized to take well under a millisecond when the machine is idle, which is where the flake lives.
const SMALL = text(25_000), LARGE = text(200_000);
const BIG_SMALL = text(250_000), BIG_LARGE = text(2_000_000);   // ten times the input, for section 3

const once = (f) => { const t = performance.now(); f(); return performance.now() - t; };
const median = (xs) => [...xs].sort((a, b) => a - b)[xs.length >> 1];
const fastest = (xs) => Math.min(...xs);
function ratio({ pick, runs, floor, warmups, interleave }, f, inputs = [SMALL, LARGE]) {
  const small = () => f(inputs[0]), large = () => f(inputs[1]);
  for (let i = 0; i < warmups; i++) { small(); large(); }
  const S = [], L = [];
  if (interleave) for (let i = 0; i < runs; i++) { S.push(once(small)); L.push(once(large)); }
  else { for (let i = 0; i < runs; i++) S.push(once(small)); for (let i = 0; i < runs; i++) L.push(once(large)); }
  return pick(L) / Math.max(pick(S), floor);   // large cost over the small cost, with the small cost floored
}
// The old arm, then each change added on top of the one before it.
const ARMS = [
  ["old: median of 3, floor 0.5, bound 20", { pick: median, runs: 3, floor: 0.5, warmups: 0, interleave: false, bound: 20 }],
  ["+ fastest of 9", { pick: fastest, runs: 9, floor: 0.5, warmups: 0, interleave: false, bound: 20 }],
  ["+ floor of 5", { pick: fastest, runs: 9, floor: 5, warmups: 0, interleave: false, bound: 20 }],
  ["+ warm-up, interleaved", { pick: fastest, runs: 9, floor: 5, warmups: 2, interleave: true, bound: 20 }],
  ["+ bound 30 (the final arm)", { pick: fastest, runs: 9, floor: 5, warmups: 2, interleave: true, bound: 30 }],
];

const burners = [];
const stopBurners = () => { for (const b of burners) b.kill("SIGKILL"); burners.length = 0; };
process.on("exit", stopBurners);
const startBurners = async (k) => {
  for (let i = 0; i < k; i++) burners.push(spawn(process.execPath, ["-e", "for(;;){}"], { stdio: "ignore" }));
  await new Promise((r) => setTimeout(r, 500));
};

// 1. False alarms: the linear scanner is correct, so every red run is a flake. Counts of red runs out of TRIALS.
console.log(`1. false alarms on a correct linear scanner, ${TRIALS} runs per cell, ${CORES} cores, busy-loop processes added:`);
const levels = [0, CORES, 2 * CORES, 4 * CORES];
const cells = ARMS.map(() => []);
for (const k of levels) {
  await startBurners(k);
  ARMS.forEach(([, arm], a) => { let red = 0; for (let t = 0; t < TRIALS; t++) if (ratio(arm, scan) >= arm.bound) red++; cells[a].push(red); });
  stopBurners();
}
console.log(["arm".padEnd(40), ...levels.map((k) => String(k).padStart(4))].join(" ") + "   <- busy loops");
ARMS.forEach(([name], a) => console.log([name.padEnd(40), ...cells[a].map((c) => String(c).padStart(4))].join(" ")));

// 2. Missed defects: a planted quadratic of decreasing severity, on an idle machine. For each arm, the ratio it computes and its verdict.
// Red is the right answer for every row except the first, which is the correct scanner.
console.log("\n2. the ratio each arm computes on an idle machine (the first row is the correct scanner):");
const oldArm = ARMS[0][1], finalArm = ARMS[4][1];
for (const share of [0, 0.005, 0.0012, 0.0006, 0.0003]) {
  const f = share === 0 ? scan : withDefect(share), a = ratio(oldArm, f), b = ratio(finalArm, f);
  const verdict = (r, arm) => (r >= arm.bound ? "red  " : "green");
  console.log(`   ${share === 0 ? "no defect    " : "severity " + String(share).padEnd(7)} old arm ${a.toFixed(1).padStart(5)} ${verdict(a, oldArm)} | final arm ${b.toFixed(1).padStart(5)} ${verdict(b, finalArm)}`);
}

// 3. The other way to a stable ratio: make the small input cost more than the floor, so no floor is needed.
// The final arm with the floor taken off (0.5), on inputs ten times larger. Counts of red runs out of 30; the scanner is still correct.
console.log("\n3. correct linear scanner, ten times the input, no floor (0.5), 30 runs per cell:");
const noFloor = { ...finalArm, floor: 0.5 }, row3 = [];
for (const k of levels) {
  await startBurners(k);
  let red = 0; for (let t = 0; t < 30; t++) if (ratio(noFloor, scan, [BIG_SMALL, BIG_LARGE]) >= noFloor.bound) red++;
  row3.push(red); stopBurners();
}
console.log(["final arm, 10x inputs, floor 0.5".padEnd(40), ...row3.map((c) => String(c).padStart(4))].join(" "));
```

Across four runs of this listing, two on Node 22.22.0 and two on Node 22.22.2, the pattern was the same every time. In section 1 the old arm was red in 1 to 4 of 100 runs at one busy loop per core, 27 to 33 at two per core and 90 to 97 at four per core. The fastest of nine was red in none, 4 to 12 and 70 to 90 at the same loads. From the floor onward, no cell was red in any run. In section 2 the old arm read between 5.9 and 11.2 on the correct scanner, even on an idle machine, and the final arm read 0.8, because its denominator is the floor. The old arm was red for all four planted defects in every run. The final arm was red for the two severe ones and green for the two mild ones in every run, and green on a planted quadratic is a miss. In section 3 no cell was red in these four runs. In one earlier run while the script was being written, the same arm had a single red run in thirty at the heaviest load, and a separate exploratory script with sixty runs per cell had one red run at eight busy loops per core, so read it as almost never red in this toy and not as a guarantee.

## What the floor can no longer see

The record states the price plainly in its own words: a planted quadratic must cost more than a threshold at the large size to be caught, and a milder superlinear cost passes, because the floor trades sensitivity for stability. The threshold is the bound times the floor.

Section 2 of the toy shows what that means. The old arm, whose floor is a fraction of a millisecond, catches all four planted defects. The final arm catches the two severe ones and misses the two mild ones, because their large-input cost sits below the bound times the floor. The final arm's reading on the correct scanner is under 1, because the floor and not the small time is its denominator, and the two mild defects read 24 and 12 against a bound of 30. The record also keeps an absolute ceiling behind an opt-in flag and a separate benchmark for the absolute measure, which is where a defect that mild would have to be caught.

## Another way to a stable ratio

The floor is needed because the small input is cheap. Section 3 of the toy takes the other route: make the inputs big enough that the small one costs more than the floor, and take the floor off. On inputs ten times larger the correct scanner stayed green at every contention level in the four runs, and had a single red run at the heaviest load in two earlier development runs. The price is ten times the work in every run. That is a result of this toy. It is not Northsun's design and the record does not recommend it.

## What this still does not prove

- The toy does not reproduce Northsun's flake or any number in the record. Its scanner, sizes, floor and load are its own, and the contention at which it flakes will differ on another machine. The record needed more than busy loops alone, and the toy needed two to four per core.
- The toy does not separate the warm-up and the interleaving from the floor. They were added after the floor, when no false alarms were left for them to fix, so no claim is made for them here.
- Why the fastest of nine still fails under heavy load is a reading and not a measurement: the toy's pattern fits long runs being slowed more than short ones, and the scheduler was not instrumented.
- A passing ratio arm shows that growth between two sizes stays under a bound. It does not show that the code is fast, or linear at other sizes.
- The record's load runs and planted quadratics are as it reports them. They were not re-run for this post, and the Northsun repository is private.

## How to check your own timing test

Five questions, in order. What is the smallest time in the ratio, and is it under a millisecond? Does the test take the fastest of several interleaved runs, or a median or a single run? Is there a floor, and what is the bound times the floor, which is the cost below which a defect passes? Have you planted a quadratic of the size you care about and watched the test go red? And have you run the test under contention you made on purpose, with more busy loops than cores, before trusting that it holds?

## FAQ

### Why not just raise the bound?

Because the bound has to sit between linear and quadratic, about eight and about sixty-four for an eightfold input, and every step upward gives up some of that gap. The failure here was a denominator problem more than a bound problem, and the toy's floor fixed it without moving the bound at all.

### Why the fastest run and not the median?

Because contention only adds time, so the fastest run is the closest observation to the code's true cost. In the toy, at two busy loops per core the fastest of nine was red in 4 to 12 runs of 100 against 27 to 33 for the median of three, and at four per core it was still red in most runs, where the floor did the work.

### How do I choose the floor?

The bound times the floor is the blind spot, so choose the floor from the smallest defect you need to catch, and back it with an absolute check for anything below that. Or make the inputs big enough that the small one never reaches the floor, at the cost of longer runs.

### Could I avoid the clock altogether?

Yes, by counting steps instead of timing them, for example comparisons or characters visited, which does not depend on the machine's load. It needs the code or the test to expose a count, and it is not what Northsun did, so this post says nothing about how well it would work.
